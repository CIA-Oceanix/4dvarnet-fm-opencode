\section{Background}\label{sec:chain}

\subsection{Bayesian data assimilation}
Let $\xv = (\xv_0, \dots, \xv_K)$ be the state trajectory over an assimilation
window, $\zv$ an external forcing and $\thv$ the physical parameters. The
discrete dynamics are given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dynamics}
  \xv_{k+1} = \Mop_\Delta(\xv_k, \zv_k; \thv) + \xi_k ,
\end{equation}
with $\Mop_\Delta$ the time-stepping operator over a physical step $\Delta$ and
$\xi_k$ a model-error process; the observations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obs}
  \yv_k = \Hop(\xv_k) + \epsv_k, \qquad \epsv_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{R}),
\end{equation}
at the observation times of the window. Everything an estimator may condition on
is collected in the \emph{context} $C = (\yv, \thv, \zv)$. The posterior
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posterior}
  p(\xv \mid C) \;\propto\; p(\yv \mid \xv)\; p(\xv \mid \thv, \zv)
\end{equation}
combines the observation likelihood (through $\Hop$ and $\mathbf{R}$) with a prior that, in model-based DA, is generated by the dynamics~\eqref{eq:dynamics}.
Learned schemes often replace or bypass this prior. Here, we focus on 
state estimation problems, meaning that $\thv$ and $\zv$ are inputs that a scheme may use. We discuss extending this approach to parameter estimation in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The conditional expectation $m = \E[\xv \mid C]$ is the associated MSE-optimal estimator, meaning that $\E[\|\xv - \E[\xv \mid C] \|^2]$ is the smallest mean-squared error any scheme can reach.

We can distinguish two main categories of model-based DA schemes to address the
estimation of $\E[\xv \mid C]$ or of the full posterior $p(\xv \mid C)$, namely
variational (4D-Var) DA schemes and ensemble Kalman ones \cite{carrassi_data_2018,cheng_machine_2023}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\bf 4D-Var schemes:} they compute the maximum a posteriori (MAP) trajectory of~\eqref{eq:posterior} under a Gaussian prior $\xv_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\xv^b, \mathbf{B})$ on the initial state and the Gaussian observation model~\eqref{eq:obs}. Minimising $-\log p(\xv \mid C)$ amounts to minimising
\begin{equation}\label{eq:4dvar}
  J(\xv) = \tfrac12 \|\xv_0 - \xv^b\|^2_{\mathbf{B}^{-1}}
  + \tfrac12 \sum_{k} \|\yv_k - \Hop(\xv_k)\|^2_{\mathbf{R}^{-1}}
  + \tfrac12 \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \|\xv_{k+1} - \Mop_\Delta(\xv_k, \zv_k;
  \thv)\|^2_{\mathbf{Q}^{-1}},
\end{equation}
where $\|\mathbf{u}\|^2_{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{A}\, \mathbf{u}$.
In the \emph{strong-constraint} form, the model is assumed perfect
($\mathbf{Q} \to 0$): the trajectory is a deterministic function of $\xv_0$,
$\xv_k = \Mop_\Delta^{k}(\xv_0)$, and the minimisation is performed over $\xv_0$
only. In the \emph{weak-constraint} form, the model-error process $\xi_k \sim
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{Q})$ of~\eqref{eq:dynamics} is retained and the whole
trajectory is optimised. The gradient of $J$ is obtained with the adjoint of
$\Mop_\Delta$ and $J$ is minimised iteratively, typically with a quasi-Newton
method such as L-BFGS. Long windows are usually split into sub-windows that are
cycled, the analysis of one providing the background of the next. 4D-Var returns
the mode of the posterior, which coincides with the conditional mean $m$ only
when the posterior is symeetric, that is for instance the case linear-Gaussian dynamics and observation
operators. Hereafter, we will consider both strong and weak 4DVar schemes, respectively referred to as S-4DVar and W-4DVar.
\item {\bf Kalman schemes:} they process observations sequentially. Assuming Gaussian errors, the filter propagates the mean and covariance of $p(\xv_k \mid
\yv_{\le k})$. The forecast step $\xv^f_k = \Mop_\Delta(\xv^a_{k-1}, \zv_{k-1}; \thv)$, $\mathbf{P}^f_k = \mathbf{M}_k \mathbf{P}^a_{k-1} \mathbf{M}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}$ is followed by the analysis step
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kalman}
  \xv^a_k = \xv^f_k + \mathbf{K}_k\big(\yv_k - \Hop(\xv^f_k)\big), \quad
  \mathbf{K}_k = \mathbf{P}^f_k \mathbf{H}_k^\top
  \big(\mathbf{H}_k \mathbf{P}^f_k \mathbf{H}_k^\top + \mathbf{R}\big)^{-1}, \quad
  \mathbf{P}^a_k = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \mathbf{H}_k)\, \mathbf{P}^f_k,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M}_k$ and $\mathbf{H}_k$ are the linearisations of $\Mop_\Delta$ and $\Hop$. This is the exact posterior for linear Gaussian systems. The
\emph{ensemble Kalman filter} (EnKF) \cite{carrassi_data_2018,evensen_data_2009} replaces $(\xv^f_k,
\mathbf{P}^f_k)$ by the empirical mean and covariance of an ensemble of $N$
members propagated with the nonlinear model, which avoids both the linearisation
and the storage of covariance matrices. The analysis update is applied to each
member, either with perturbed observations (stochastic EnKF) or through a
deterministic square-root transform of the ensemble anomalies (ETKF). It is
usually complemented by covariance inflation, to compensate for sampling errors
and model error, and by localisation. Ensemble smoothers (EnKS, ETKS) apply the
same update to a whole window, so that the state at each time is conditioned on
all observations of the window and not only on the past ones. The ensemble
provides samples that are used as an approximation of the posterior, with the
Gaussian assumption in the analysis step. Hereafter, we will focus on ETKF, EnKF and ETKS schemes as representative of the Kalman DA category. 
\end{itemize}

Regarding neural DA schemes, we can distinguish deterministic end-to-end schemes
aiming at approximating $\E[\xv \mid C]$ and probabilistic approaches aiming at
approximating or sampling the full posterior $p(\xv \mid C)$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\bf Deterministic neural schemes} train a neural operator $\Psi_{\Theta}$ that maps
the context $C$ to a state estimate, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:neural_mean}
  \forall C, \quad \Psi_{\Theta^*}(C) \approx \E[\xv \mid C].
\end{equation}
The conditional mean being the MSE-optimal estimator, the associated training
loss is the mean squared error over training pairs $(\xv, C)$,
\begin{equation}
  \Theta^* = \arg\min_{\Theta}\; \E\big[\|\Psi_\Theta(C) - \xv\|^2\big],
\end{equation}
where the expectation is taken over the joint distribution of states and
contexts, hence over the observing systems, parameters and forcings seen at
training. Once trained, a single forward
pass replaces the iterative minimisation of 4D-Var or the ensemble
propagation of Kalman schemes. U-Net and transformer architectures are
state-of-the-art parametric families classically used for reconstruction
problems on $n$-dimensional tensors \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}. The operator only sees what it was
trained on: if some components of $C$ (for example $\thv$ or $\zv$) are not
provided as inputs, the network learns the conditional mean marginalised over
them, and the estimate degrades when the observing system leaves the training
distribution. Hereafter, we will focus on UNet architctures and refer to this subcategory of neural schemes as M-Unet. 
\item {\bf Probabilistic neural schemes.} use generative models, such as diffusion models
\cite{song_score-based_2021} and flow matching \cite{lipman_flow_2023}, to sample the posterior rather than to
estimate its mean. Using the linear path~\eqref{eq:path} between a noise sample
$\xv_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and a target state $\xv_1$, conditional flow
matching learns the velocity field $v_\Theta(\xv_\tau, \tau, C)$ by regression,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cfm}
  \Theta^* = \arg\min_\Theta\; \E\Big[\big\|v_\Theta(\xv_\tau, \tau, C) -
  (\xv_1 - \xv_0)\big\|^2\Big],
\end{equation}
whose minimiser is $v(\xv_\tau, \tau, C) = \E[\xv_1 - \xv_0 \mid \xv_\tau, C]
= \big(\Psi(\xv_\tau, \tau, C) - \xv_\tau\big)/(1-\tau)$, with $\Psi =
\E[\xv_1 \mid \xv_\tau, C]$ the denoiser of the posterior at noise level
$\tau$. Samples of $p(\xv \mid C)$ are then generated by integrating the ODE
$\dot{\xv}_\tau = v_\Theta(\xv_\tau, \tau, C)$ from $\tau=0$ to $\tau=1$, for
instance with a few Euler steps, and an ensemble is obtained by repeating the
draw from different noise samples. At $\tau=0$, $\Psi$ reduces to the
conditional mean~\eqref{eq:neural_mean}, so that the deterministic schemes are
the mean part of this representation and the flow adds the uncertainty on top
of it. This conditional setting, in which the full context $C$ is an input of
the network, is the direct counterpart of the deterministic scheme above. Hereafter, we will focus on this conditional flow matching referred to as CFM.
A second route does not condition on the observations at training time. The
generative model is trained to represent the dynamical prior $p(\xv \mid \thv,
\zv)$ only, and the posterior~\eqref{eq:posterior} is obtained at inference by
\emph{observation-guided sampling} \cite{chung_diffusion_2022,rozet_score-based_2023,daras_survey_2024}: the likelihood term is injected into
the sampler through the known observation model~\eqref{eq:obs} similarly to model-based DA schemes. Because the guided
likelihood is only approximate (Gaussian approximation, tempering), the
resulting samples approximate the true posterior only up to a structural error.
Following \cite{rozet_score-based_2023}, we will refer to this class of neural approaches as score-based data assimilation schemes (SDA). 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The estimation error chain for \texorpdfstring{$\E[\xv \mid C]$}{E[x|C]}}

Let us focus here on the skills of a DA scheme in terms of estimation of the conditional mean $m_C=\E[\xv \mid C]$. The DA scheme  departs from this MSE-optimal estimation in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item the DA scheme may use some information
$S \subseteq C$ rather than the full context $C$: for instance, contrary to a smoother, a DA filter uses the observations up to the current time; a learned DA scheme may ignore $\thv$ and $\zv$;
\item It believes a model $\tilde p( \cdot|C)$ that may differ from the true posterior $p( \cdot|C)$: for instance, 4DVar schemes relate to considering the MAP estimate and Ensemble Kalman methods exploit Gaussian approximation, which can be interpreted as not considering the true posterior $p$;
\item It computes its own target only approximately: this accounts among other for the number of optimization steps, finite-ensemble approximations, training dataset size\ldots 
\end{itemize}
This leads to the corresponding chain of estimators as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
  m = \E[\xv\mid C] \;\longrightarrow\; m_S = \E[\xv\mid S]
  \;\longrightarrow\; \tilde m = \E_{\tilde p}[\xv\mid S]
  \;\longrightarrow\; \hat m ,
\end{equation}
where $\hat m$ is what the DA scheme outputs. Assuming that estimate $\hat m$ depends only on subcontext $S$ and on randomness independent of the truth, we can derive the following decomposition of the associated mean square estimation error $ \E \|\hat m - \xv\|^2$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain0}
  \E \|\hat m - \xv\|^2 \;=\;
  \underbrace{\E\tr\Cov(\xv\mid C)}_{\text{irreducible}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\E\|m - m_S\|^2}_{\text{restriction}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\E\|m_S - \hat m\|^2}_{\text{misspecification+approximation }} %.  \;+\; \underbrace{\E\|\tilde m - \hat m\|^2}_{\text{}} .
\end{equation}
The first holds because $m$ is the conditional expectation
given $C$. The second holds because $m_S = \E[m \mid S]$ (tower property). The third holds because $m_S - \tilde m$ is a function of $S$ and of independent randomness only, so the cross terms vanish. The latter also applies to the fourth term. One cannot in general split the third term into distinct mispecification and approximation terms, as their conditional independence w.r.t. $S$ is not always verified. 

%The last term contains the misspecification $\E\|m_S - \tilde m\|^2$, the approximation $\E\|\tilde m - \hat m\|^2$ and their cross term. That cross term does not vanish in general: a finite ensemble can partly compensate a wrong model, or worsen it. We therefore do not claim a four-term identity. We separate misspecification from approximation by controlled contrasts --- the same scheme with more members, more iterations or more training, and the same scheme with and without model error (Sect.~\ref{sec:design}) --- and we report the cross term as the residual it is.

We can analyse the design choices in the above-mentioned model-based and neural schemes according to the associated error term as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:commitments}. These features are choices, not errors: each buys something --- tractability,
a sequential implementation, robustness to the observing system --- and the
question is what it costs, and when the cost changes nature. This synthesis emphasizes that all model-based DA schemes involve a non-zero mispecification error. By contrast, end-to-end neural schemes (M-Unet, CFM) may just involve approximation error terms given their versatility to embed any type of conditioning and universal approximation theorem for state-of-the-art neural network families \cite{hornik_multilayer_1989,cybenko_approximation_1989}. This likely provides first insights to support why neural schemes can outperform model-based DA schemes when dealing with in-distribution generalization \cite{fablet_learning_2021,boudier_dan_2020}, {\em i.e.} when the test data fall within the same distribution as the training data, which is usually the case in idealized evaluation benchmark \cite{brajard_combining_2021,fablet_learning_2021,rozet_score-based_2023}. Beyond this idealized configuration, one can obviously question how the mispecification term grows for neural schemes under out-of-distribution inference compared to that of model-based DA ones.
