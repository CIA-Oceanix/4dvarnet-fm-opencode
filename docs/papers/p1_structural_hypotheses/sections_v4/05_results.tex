\subsection{Error budget with a perfect model}\label{sec:q1}\label{sec:q1-budget}
\paragraph{Overall performance.}
Table~\ref{tab:s0} reports the performance of all schemes with a perfect model. On L96,
the learned schemes outperform model-based DA. D-Unet+SDA is best for both the mean and
the distribution (EV 0.981, CRPSS 0.908), followed by CFM (EV 0.977, CRPSS 0.901) and
D-Unet (EV 0.972). The best DA scheme, the ETKS (EV 0.866, CRPSS 0.744), is behind
every learned scheme, including SDA (EV 0.923, CRPSS 0.790), and the 4D-Var schemes rank
last (EV 0.74--0.76), behind the ensemble filters (0.80--0.82). These differences come
almost entirely from the fast variables. On the 8 slow variables, all schemes reach an
EV of 0.96--0.99, and the ETKS (0.988) is within 0.01 of the best scheme (0.995). On the
16 observed fast variables, the DA schemes reach 0.63--0.80, against 0.87--0.94 for the
learned schemes. On
QG, every scheme explains more than 97\,\% of the climatological variance of the
streamfunction, and the differences lie in the remaining normalised MSE. The learned schemes
lead for the mean (EV 0.9935--0.9951, D-Unet+SDA best) and reduce the normalised MSE of
Strong-4DVar, the best DA scheme, by 27 to 45\,\% (0.0049--0.0065 against 0.0089), followed by
the ETKS (EV 0.9868). For the distribution, the three sample-based learned schemes are on par (CRPSS
0.927--0.929), ahead of the ETKS (0.898) and the filters (0.86--0.87). In both case studies, smoothing improves on
filtering, and the ETKF slightly outperforms the EnKF. On L63, which is low-dimensional and
densely observed, every scheme explains more than 98.8\,\% of the variance; D-Unet+SDA is best
for the mean and the distribution (EV 0.9980, CRPSS 0.957), and the DA schemes are within 0.006
of it (ETKS 0.9947, Weak-4DVar 0.9957). The spread--skill ratios qualify these rankings on L96
(for the learned schemes, values of the 1D backbone; the 2D ones are pending): CFM is nearly
calibrated (SS 0.93), whereas the DA ensembles are
over-confident, the more so as they are more accurate (EnKF 0.85, ETKF 0.76, ETKS 0.48).
SDA is moderately over-confident (0.81), and D-Unet+SDA, despite the best mean and CRPSS,
markedly so (0.56), a consequence of its tempered guidance (Sect.~\ref{sec:q3}). On L63, by
contrast, the filters are over-dispersed at their RMSE-optimal inflation (SS 1.76--1.77), the
smoother is close to calibrated (0.86), and the learned ensembles are over-confident
(0.57--0.71).
On QG, the DA ensembles are over-confident too (SS 0.71 for the ETKF and EnKF, 0.64 for the
ETKS), CFM is the closest to calibrated (0.84), and D-Unet+SDA is again the most
over-confident (0.37). The following paragraphs analyse
where the gaps to the best scheme come from.

\paragraph{Irreducible component.}
The best scheme sets the floor of each case study: D-Unet+SDA on L96 (normalised
mean-squared error $1-\mathrm{EV} = 0.019$) and on QG (0.0049). It is only
an upper bound on the irreducible term $\mathrm{mmse}$ in~\eqref{eq:chain0}: we have no lower bound, so every component below is a share of the excess over this floor, not over the true
irreducible error.

\paragraph{Restriction component in filtering schemes.}
A filter conditions each state on past observations only, a smoother on the whole
window. Measured with large ensembles, so that the ensemble size does not contaminate
it, filtering costs 0.057 of normalised MSE on L96 (ETKF 0.141 against ETKS 0.084,
100 members) and 0.011 on QG (0.020 against 0.010, 320 members). It is the largest
identified term of the DA ensembles in both case studies. To check that this gain is a
restriction, we run the filter and the smoother on the same L96 ensemble realisation
and evaluate both sides of the identity
$\mathrm{MSE}_F - \mathrm{MSE}_S = \mathrm{var}_F - \mathrm{var}_S = \E\|m_F - m_S\|^2$
(physical units). It holds approximately with 100 members --- an MSE gap of 0.161
against a variance gap of 0.176 and a mean shift of 0.241 --- and less well with 30
(0.159, 0.227, 0.296). The deviation shrinks as the ensemble grows, as expected if it
is an approximation error of the ensemble.

\paragraph{Approximation components.} We can partly assess the approximation components of the different schemes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Ensemble size in ensemble DA approaches.} Going from 30 to 100 members reduces the normalised MSE
    of the L96 ETKS from 0.134 to 0.084 (37\,\%) and that of the ETKF from 0.185 to
    0.141. On QG, going from 80 to 320 members (with a re-tuned localisation radius)
    reduces it from 0.013 to 0.010 for the ETKS and from 0.024 to 0.020 for the ETKF.
  \item \textbf{Optimisation in 4DVar schemes.} With 10 L-BFGS iterations per sub-window,
    Strong-4DVar is far from its own optimum (validation RMSE 0.721 against 0.545 with
    40 iterations). The benchmark uses 40 iterations, at which the solution has
    converged, so the remaining excess of 4D-Var is not an optimisation error.
  \item \textbf{Training setup and architecture design in neural schemes.} Training D-Unet 
  for 1200 instead of 400 epochs reduces its L96 RMSE from 0.380 to 0.340, and three times more training windows only to 0.334: the approximation term of the learned schemes is close to saturation at our budget.
  Complementary experiments with three tiers of the shared U-Net backbone, under an
  earlier protocol (fixed observing system, 400 epochs, one seed;
  Appendix~\ref{app:tiers}), support that network capacity is not the limiting
  approximation term: a larger tier (23.5\,M parameters) brings no gain, while a
  smaller one with four times fewer parameters (1.5\,M) increases the RMSE by 4 to
  24\,\% (e.g. 0.490 vs 0.470 for D-Unet and 0.410 vs 0.341 for CFM on L96).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Residual component.}
The excess left after these contrasts is a residual: in the budget~\eqref{eq:residual}, it
contains the misspecification of the scheme, the cross term and the approximation that the
resources we vary do not remove. Since the floor is the best scheme and not the MMSE, it is
measured as an excess over the floor, i.e.\ a lower bound of the residual with respect to the
irreducible term. On L96, it is 0.065 for the 100-member ETKS; part of it may be the floor that a
Gaussian update leaves above the MMSE for any ensemble size~\cite{fablet_not_2026}, which our
contrasts do not identify. It makes up almost all of the excess of the 4D-Var schemes
(0.22 to 0.24), although they use the exact model and have converged. Its candidates, which our
contrasts do not separate, are the
mode instead of the mean, whose gap to the mean does not depend on the
resources~\cite{fablet_not_2026}, the hard constraint and the Gaussian background; cycling over
sub-windows adds a restriction that no contrast measures here. The EnKF exceeds the ETKF (0.199 against 0.185), consistent with the
sampling noise of its perturbed observations. For the neural schemes, the residual
term is smaller: 0.009 for D-Unet, 0.004 for CFM and 0.058 for SDA, whose guided sampler
approximates the likelihood. On QG, the same holds at a smaller scale: the residual
is 0.005 for the 320-member ETKS, the EnKF again exceeds the ETKF (0.027 against 0.024),
and the neural schemes lie within 0.002 of the floor. The excess of Strong-4DVar is entirely
residual, as on L96, but small on the streamfunction (0.004). Its structural costs show more
clearly on the potential vorticity, which is smaller-scale and unobserved: the learned schemes
reach an EV of 0.76--0.79 there, against 0.62 for Strong-4DVar and the ETKS.

\paragraph{Synthesis.}
Figure~\ref{fig:budget-s0} summarises the budget per scheme family. For the ensemble
DA schemes, the excess over the floor splits into comparable shares of restriction,
approximation and residual error on L96 (ETKF: 0.057, 0.044 and 0.065), while the
restriction due to filtering dominates on QG (ETKF: 0.011, 0.004 and 0.005). For the
variational schemes, the excess is almost entirely residual in both case studies, but
small on QG (0.004), where Strong-4DVar is the best DA scheme. By contrast, the end-to-end neural schemes,
D-Unet and CFM, lie just above the floor in both case studies (excess 0.009 and 0.004
on L96, 0.0006 and 0.0017 on QG). SDA, which combines a learned prior with an explicit
likelihood, has a larger excess on L96 (0.058), in the same range as the residual
of the 100-member ETKS (0.065). This points to a misspecification of the
posterior induced by the approximate likelihood of the guided sampler, even though the
observation operator and the error statistics are exact. On QG, by contrast, SDA guided
from pure noise lies within 0.001 of the floor, and started from the D-Unet estimate it
sets it (D-Unet+SDA): this excess is therefore not intrinsic to guided sampling. Overall, even with a perfect dynamical model, the error
budget of the DA schemes involves significant approximation and misspecification
components, beyond the restriction due to their conditioning, as expected
from~\cite{fablet_not_2026}: a Gaussian update leaves a floor above the MMSE whatever the
ensemble size, and a posterior mode a gap to the mean whatever the iterations, whereas learned
estimators of the window escape the first. These components prevent
the schemes from extracting the full information that the observations carry about
the state: an assessment of an observing system through a model-based DA system measures what
that system extracts, which can be less than what the observations allow, as the learned
schemes show here. This is distinct from the known overestimation of observation impact by
identical-twin OSSEs, in which the same model generates the truth and assimilates the
observations~\cite{halliwell_rigorous_2014,yu_evaluation_2019}: there, the bias comes from the experimental design; here, the observing system and
the truth are fixed, and the comparison is between estimators.

\paragraph{Overall performance.}
Table~\ref{tab:s1} reports the performance under model error. On L96, model-based DA
degrades strongly: the EV of the ETKF, EnKF, ETKS and Strong-4DVar falls to 0.24--0.36,
and the CRPSS of the DA ensembles to about 0.2. Weak-4DVar is the best DA scheme (EV
0.561). The degradation is again much larger on the fast variables: the slow variables
keep an EV of 0.61--0.67 (0.86 for Weak-4DVar), while the fast ones fall to 0.03--0.23
(0.41 for Weak-4DVar). The DA ensembles also become more over-confident: their
spread--skill ratio drops to 0.59 for the ETKF, 0.72 for the EnKF and 0.34 for the ETKS,
while those of the learned schemes change little (1D backbone: CFM 0.93 to 0.94, D-Unet+SDA
0.56 to 0.58), except SDA, whose prior receives the biased parameters (0.81 to 0.95). On L63, model
error lowers the EV of the DA schemes to 0.938--0.992 (Strong-4DVar lowest, Weak-4DVar highest),
while the learned schemes keep 0.992--0.997; the filters become more over-dispersed (ETKF SS
2.86).
On QG, the DA schemes keep an EV of 0.950--0.971, with Weak-4DVar best for the mean
(0.9711) and the ETKS best for the distribution (CRPSS 0.819). With the observation-error
variance inflated for \So, their ensembles are close to calibrated on the streamfunction (SS
0.84--0.93), unlike on L96. The learned schemes are almost unaffected (L96 EV 0.924--0.982;
QG 0.988--0.990 for D-Unet, CFM and D-Unet+SDA), with D-Unet+SDA again best for the mean and
the best distribution given by D-Unet+SDA on L96 (CRPSS 0.909) and CFM on QG (0.896); on QG,
however, they become over-confident (SS 0.72 for CFM, 0.27 for D-Unet+SDA). SDA guided from
pure noise is the exception on QG: its EV falls to 0.9728, and that of the potential vorticity
from 0.76 to 0.35. Its likelihood assumes white observation errors of 5\,\%, whereas the \So{}
errors are 15\,\% white and 15\,\% correlated per pass: a misspecification of the likelihood,
not of the dynamical model. For L63 and L96, the \So{} test data remain
within the training distribution of the neural schemes, since only the model given to
DA changes. For QG, the \So{} observation errors (15\,\% white and 15\,\% correlated
per pass) lie at the upper edge of the noise range used to train D-Unet (5--15\,\%
white, 0--15\,\% correlated), while the SDA prior does not see the observations, which
enter only through its likelihood. The QG experiments thus also test the robustness of
the neural schemes to observation errors at the limit of their training distribution.

\paragraph{Impacts of misspecification.}\label{sec:q2-represent}
The increase of the normalised MSE from \Sz{} to \So{} measures misspecification plus
the cross term of the chain (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget-s1}, hatched red), and its effect
differs between scheme families. For the ensemble DA schemes, the increase is
0.50--0.54 on L96, i.e. about three times the \Sz{} error of the ETKF and EnKF and nearly
four times that of the ETKS, and 0.019--0.024 on QG, i.e. one to one and a half times
their \Sz{} error. The contrasts that reduced their
\Sz{} excess no longer help, as expected of a misspecification, which by definition remains
with unlimited resources~\cite{fablet_not_2026}: 100 members leave the L96 ETKS unchanged (EV 0.358 with 30
and 0.359 with 100 members), 320 members barely improve the QG ETKS (EV 0.9674 to 0.9681), and smoothing
gains only 12\,\% of normalised MSE over filtering on L96 (0.642 against 0.728). On QG,
smoothing keeps most of its \Sz{} gain (32\,\%, 0.033 against 0.048, against 45\,\% at
\Sz). The filter/smoother identity also fails: on L96, the
MSE gap is 0.237, against a variance gap of 0.534 and a mean shift of 0.913. Re-tuning
the inflation (L96) or the observation-error variance (QG) for \So{} recovers only part
of the loss, e.g. from EV 0.9603 to 0.9674 for the QG ETKS (normalised MSE 0.040 to
0.033). For the variational schemes,
the hard constraint is the least robust: the increase is 0.50 on L96 and 0.034 on QG,
the largest of the QG DA schemes, where Strong-4DVar goes from the best DA scheme with a
perfect model to behind the ETKS and Weak-4DVar under model error; on the potential
vorticity, its EV falls from 0.62 to 0.17. A weak constraint, with its model-error scale tuned per case, gains
little at \Sz{} (EV 0.757 against 0.741 on L96), as expected, but reduces the normalised
MSE of Strong-4DVar by 42\,\% on L96 (0.439 against 0.761) and 33\,\% on QG (0.029
against 0.043) at \So{}, making Weak-4DVar the best DA scheme for the mean. It remains,
however, about twenty times the learned bound on L96: an additive per-step model error with
a scalar scale does not absorb the model error of this experiment, which is dominated by the
truncation of the DA model (Sect.~\ref{sec:design}). By contrast, the neural schemes are
barely affected: D-Unet, CFM, SDA and D-Unet+SDA are flat on L96 (within 0.001 of normalised
MSE). The SDA row is the unconditional SDA1 prior until SDA2 is trained with the 2D backbone; with
the 1D backbone, the parameter-conditioned SDA2, which receives the biased parameters, lost 0.005
(Sect.~\ref{sec:q3}). On QG, D-Unet, CFM and D-Unet+SDA lose about 0.005 of normalised MSE, the effect of the
\So{} observation errors, the only part of \So{} they see; SDA guided from pure noise loses
0.021, through its misspecified likelihood.

\paragraph{Synthesis.}\label{sec:q2-oracle}
Under model error, misspecification dominates the budget of every model-based scheme, with
the model-error treatments considered here (inflation, an inflated observation error, a weak
constraint), and the restriction and approximation terms of \Sz{} become secondary
(Fig.~\ref{fig:budget-s1}). The observations still bring information to the
model-based schemes: on QG, a free run of the biased model explains 66\,\% of the
variance of the streamfunction (EV 0.66), against 0.95--0.97 after assimilation. But misspecification
caps what they can extract, and the explicit representations of model uncertainty
available to DA (a weak constraint, inflation, an inflated observation error) recover
only part of it. The neural schemes, trained on simulations of the true system and running no dynamical model
at inference time, do not see the misspecification of the model given to DA, by construction: on
L96 they
reach an EV above 0.9 from the same observations, against at most 0.56 for DA. This gap
is, however, an upper bound on what the observations allow under model error: the
neural schemes are trained on simulations of the true system, so the gap also contains
the value of that training data. \needsrun{Neural schemes trained on simulations of the
biased model, with $\sigma = 0$ and 20\,\%; their gap to the rows trained on the true
system measures what training on reanalyses or true-system data implicitly brings.}
Under model error, the skill of a DA scheme therefore reflects the fidelity of its
model as much as the information content of the observations, which further limits
its use to assess an observing system.

\paragraph{Skill of the reported posterior.}
Figure~\ref{fig:fmss} extends the explained variance of Tables~\ref{tab:s0}
and~\ref{tab:s1} ($\tau = 0$) to the reported distribution ($\tau > 0$). With a perfect
model, all schemes lose skill as $\tau$ grows, but at different rates. The DA ensembles
fall from 0.80--0.87 to 0.41--0.50 at $\tau = 0.75$, and their ranking reverses: the ETKS,
best for the mean, still leads at $\tau = 0.5$ (0.79 against 0.75 for the ETKF) but ends
below both filters (0.41 against 0.50 and 0.49). CFM keeps most of its skill (0.94 to
0.80) and reaches the level of D-Unet+SDA (0.80) at $\tau = 0.75$, although its mean is
less accurate. Reporting no uncertainty is penalised: D-Unet, as accurate as the best
learned schemes for the mean, falls to 0.55, and Strong-4DVar to $-1.6$. Under model
error, the decay of the DA schemes becomes much steeper: their FMSS crosses zero, i.e.
the climatological forecast, as soon as $\tau \approx 0.5$, and reaches $-1.56$ for the
ETKS at $\tau = 0.75$, while their mean keeps a positive skill (EV 0.27--0.36). The learned
schemes keep their \Sz{} curves. Part of these negative values is the normalisation:
$\mathrm{FMSS}_\tau$ compares a scheme with climatology at the same $\tau$, whose score shrinks
as $1/(1+\alpha_\tau)$, so that an estimate reporting no uncertainty, with normalised MSE $e$,
has $\mathrm{FMSS}_\tau = 1 - (1+\alpha_\tau)\,e$ (e.g.\ $-1.6$ for Strong-4DVar at \Sz). They
say that the reported distributions are worse than climatology at large $\tau$, not that model
error costs more on the distribution than on the mean. On QG, the skill of the
reported distribution stays high for every scheme, as the streamfunction is smooth and
well observed, but the same ordering holds. With a perfect model, the learned schemes lead
at $\tau = 0.75$ (0.94--0.95), ahead of Strong-4DVar (0.91, no uncertainty), the ETKS
(0.89) and the filters (0.82--0.84). Under model error, the DA ensembles fall to
0.71--0.78 and Strong-4DVar to 0.57, while D-Unet, CFM and D-Unet+SDA stay at 0.89--0.90;
SDA guided from pure noise, with its misspecified likelihood, falls to 0.78. \todo{L63.}

\paragraph{Model error along the flow time.} Skill scores at different $\tau$ are normalised by
different climatological scores, which shrink as $\tau$ grows: the comparison above is relative to
climatology at each $\tau$, and it does not say whether model error costs more on the
distribution than on the mean. Figure~\ref{fig:missp-tau} answers this in raw scores, with the
increase $\Delta\mathrm{FMS}_\tau$ from \Sz{} to \So{} of each scheme, normalised by its value
at $\tau = 0$, i.e.\ by the increase of the mean-squared error. A scheme that reports no
uncertainty has a ratio of 1 at every $\tau$. For every ensemble, the ratio decreases with $\tau$ (0.12--0.39 at $\tau = 0.75$ for the L96
DA ensembles, 0.50--0.60 for the QG DA ensembles and 0.77--0.98 for the QG learned schemes): in
raw scores, model error does not cost more on the reported distribution than on the mean. This
ratio alone, however, does not show that the reported spread represents the model error: in the
Gaussian form, $\mathrm{FMS}_\tau$ grows with the squared error of the mean at the rate
$1/(1+\alpha_\tau v)^2 < 1$ for any non-zero spread $v$, so that it falls below 1 for any
ensemble, calibrated or not. Pairing the \So{} means of the L96 DA ensembles with their \Sz{}
spreads, the ratio at $\tau = 0.75$ is 0.70--0.84 instead of 0.12--0.39: the Gaussian slope alone keeps
it below 1, and the larger \So{} spreads, from the re-tuned inflation, account for the rest of the
decrease. Whether the spread represents the model error is shown instead by its calibration and
by the share of the mean in the score. The lower panels split the remaining increase: the share removed by the
best scalar rescaling of the spread grows with $\tau$ for the L96 DA ensembles, up to 71\,\% for
the ETKS at $\tau = 0.75$, whose spread remains mis-scaled under model error despite its re-tuned
inflation. On QG, the six-fold observation-error variance leaves the DA ensembles calibrated
under model error (negative shares), whereas a growing share of the increase of the learned
schemes (8 to 18\,\%) is a loss of calibration. The rest combines the error of the mean with the
resolution of the spread (Sect.~\ref{sec:fms}). Bounding the mean part of the score separates
the two (Table~\ref{tab:mean-share}). On QG, the mean part is at least two thirds of the score at
$\tau = 0.75$ for every scheme and in both experiments (at least 59\,\% for SDA under model
error): the reported distributions are scored mostly through their mean, and so is the cost of
model error. On L96, the distribution weighs more. With a perfect model, the mean part of the ETKS
score is 33 to 66\,\%, and under model error at most 37\,\%: most of the large-$\tau$ score of
the smoother is then the characterisation of its distribution, not its mean, consistent with the
calibration share of Fig.~\ref{fig:missp-tau}. The ETKF and EnKF keep a mean part of 41 to
88\,\%, and the learned ensembles of 60 to 100\,\%, in both experiments. D-Unet, which reports no
uncertainty, scores its mean-squared error at every $\tau$, of which a Gaussian with its mean and an
error-sized variance would keep only 29\,\% on L96.

\paragraph{Calibration and shape.}
The $\tau$-resolved spread--skill ratio (Fig.~\ref{fig:ss-tau}) and the shape diagnostic (Fig.~\ref{fig:g-tau}) explain these differences. CFM is calibrated at every $\tau$ and in both experiments
    ($\mathrm{SS}_\tau$ 0.92--0.95). The DA ensembles are over-confident, and the more so
    the more accurate their mean: at \Sz{}, $\mathrm{SS}_{0.75}$ is 0.56 for the ETKF,
    0.59 for the EnKF and 0.39 for the ETKS, and at \So{} it drops to 0.54, 0.63 and at
    most 0.35. The smoother thus buys its better mean with an over-confident ensemble, and
    model error worsens it. D-Unet+SDA is uniformly over-confident ($\mathrm{SS}_\tau
    \approx 0.56$), the cost of its tempered guidance, while SDA is close to calibrated at
    small $\tau$ and over-confident at large $\tau$ (0.64 at \Sz). A calibrated pooled
    spread does not imply a calibrated distribution: on the random observing system at
    \So, the ETKF has a pooled spread--skill ratio of 0.99, yet a scalar rescaling still
    removes 12\,\% of its score at $\tau = 0.75$, as its spread does not match its error
    where the error occurs. Regarding the shape of the distribution, the diagnostic has a
    small negative floor: on synthetic Gaussian posteriors, 30 members drawn from the true
    distribution give $G_{0.75}$ between $0$ and $-1.4\,\%$, and over-confident Gaussian
    members down to $-4\,\%$, with no shape effect at all. Within this floor, the shape
    of the DA ensembles at \Sz{} ($-4.5\,\%$ for the ETKF, $-5.2\,\%$ for the ETKS) cannot
    be distinguished from the effect of their over-confidence. Under model error, by
    contrast, their shape is clearly misleading ($-22\,\%$ for the ETKS, $-28\,\%$ for the
    ETKF): their members are short-tailed with respect to their errors, so that the truth
    falls outside the ensemble more often than its spread allows. The nearly calibrated
    neural ensembles show a small but genuine gain ($G_{0.75}$ of $+2$ to $+3\,\%$ for CFM
    and SDA): their shape carries some information beyond their mean and variance.
On QG, the picture differs in two ways. First, the shape diagnostic is negligible for every
scheme ($|G_\tau| < 0.6\,\%$): on the streamfunction, the members carry no information
beyond their mean and variance, and the DA ensembles are not short-tailed. Second,
calibration depends on the experiment. With a perfect model, all ensembles are
over-confident ($\mathrm{SS}_\tau$ 0.63--0.75 for the DA ensembles, 0.73--0.84 for CFM and
SDA, 0.37 for D-Unet+SDA). Under model error, the DA ensembles, run with a six-fold
observation-error variance, are close to calibrated ($\mathrm{SS}_\tau$ 0.84--1.06; a scalar
rescaling removes at most 1.4\,\% of their score at $\tau = 0.75$), whereas the learned
schemes, which see \So{} only through its observation errors, become over-confident
($\mathrm{SS}_\tau$ 0.71--0.72 for CFM, 0.50--0.63 for SDA and 0.27--0.30 for D-Unet+SDA,
with calibration losses of 4, 14 and 11\,\% at $\tau = 0.75$). \todo{L63.}

\paragraph{Synthesis.}
The $\tau$-resolved scores separate what the mean-based metrics of Sects.~\ref{sec:q1}
and~\ref{sec:q2} conflate. With a perfect model, on L96 and QG, the DA ensembles lose more skill from
the mean to the distribution than the learned schemes, because they are over-confident,
and the more so as their mean improves; smoothing and larger ensembles reduce the error
of the mean, not the miscalibration. Under model error, raw scores do not show a larger cost on the
distribution than on the mean, but on L96 the reported spread of the DA ensembles does not
represent the model error: they become more over-confident and, beyond the noise floor of the
shape diagnostic, short-tailed, a scalar rescaling of their spread removes up to 63\,\% of the
score of the smoother at $\tau = 0.75$, and their skill for the distribution falls below that
of climatology. The learned schemes, by
contrast, report a distribution whose skill, calibration and shape are unchanged from \Sz{} to
\So{} on L96, with CFM the best calibrated. On
QG, the inflated observation-error variance of the \So{} ensembles reverses the ordering
of calibration: an explicit error model that can be re-tuned keeps the reported spread of
DA calibrated, whereas the learned schemes, at the edge of the error statistics of their
training data, become over-confident.
These conclusions hold when the score is evaluated on random projections of space--time
patches rather than per element (Appendix~\ref{app:fms}), which accounts for the
dependence between variables. The filter/smoother identity of Sect.~\ref{sec:q1} extends along
$\tau$, with the noised state added to both contexts (Proposition~\ref{prop:restriction}): the gap of
scores, the gap of the reported $\mathrm{mmse}_q(\tau)$ and the shift of the operators should coincide. On L96
with a perfect model and 100 members, they remain of the same order and shrink together as $\tau$ grows
(0.057, 0.062 and 0.085 at $\tau = 0$; 0.023, 0.036 and 0.049 at $\tau = 0.5$; standardised units).
Under model error, the score gap changes sign from $\tau = 0.5$ ($-0.049$, against 0.132 and 0.206):
at large $\tau$, the filter scores better than the smoother, whose reported distribution is the more
over-confident.

\paragraph{Same likelihood, two priors.}
With a perfect model and the 1D backbone, the learned joint-window prior (SDA, EV 0.908--0.911)
outperforms
the model-derived priors of the hard constraint (Strong-4DVar, 0.741) and of the
30-member smoother (ETKS, 0.866), although these use the exact equations. It does not
outperform the 100-member smoother (0.916); with the 2D backbone, the unconditional prior
already exceeds it (SDA1, 0.923, Table~\ref{tab:prior}), so that this crossover depends on the
backbone \needsrun{SDA2 and SDA3 on the 2D backbone}. Here, matched likelihood means the same
observation operator and error covariance, not the same inference: guided sampling, Gaussian
updates and the posterior mode approximate the posterior differently. The ordering is set by the approximation term
of the model-based scheme, not by the fidelity of its prior, which is exact at \Sz{}. The
learned prior also gives the better reported distribution ($\mathrm{FMSS}_{0.75}$ of
0.62--0.69 against 0.41 for the ETKS), as it is less over-confident (SS 0.61--0.81
against 0.48); with 100 members, the ETKS reaches the same skill for the distribution (0.63), while
remaining over-confident (SS 0.56).

\paragraph{A better prior is not a better estimate.}
Two observations make the point. Training the SDA prior three times longer (400 to 1200
epochs) improves the estimate by 7 to 9\,\% in RMSE, and the true equations, used as a hard
prior, give the worst DA scheme at \Sz. What matters is how much of the prior the scheme
can actually use within its approximation budget. This reading also resolves the
controlled result that motivated us~\cite{fablet_learning_2021}: a learned prior can beat
the true model because of the approximation and restriction terms of the scheme that uses
the true model, not because the learned prior is better.

\paragraph{Conditioning the prior.}
Conditioning the learned prior on the window parameters $\thv$ and forcing brings little
with a perfect model: SDA1, which ignores them, reaches an EV of 0.908, against 0.910 and
0.911 for SDA2 and SDA3, i.e. 2--3\,\% of normalised MSE. Under model error, this
conditioning becomes a misspecification channel when the prior was trained with exact
parameters: SDA2, given the biased parameters, loses 0.005 of EV and leaks the bias into
the D-Unet+SDA scheme built on it (RMSE 0.310 to 0.333 with 400-epoch priors), whereas SDA3,
trained with noisy parameters, is unaffected. \needsrun{Value of the conditioning
information for DA and SDA: the same test windows with nominal parameters and/or no forcing
given to the schemes (ETKF, EnKF, ETKS, Strong-4DVar with the full model; SDA2, SDA3).}

\paragraph{Composing a mean and an uncertainty.}
In the operator language of Sect.~\ref{sec:fms}, D-Unet+SDA composes D-Unet's mean with
the anomaly of SDA3's guided sampler. It has the best mean in both experiments (EV 0.961),
but not the best distribution: CFM reaches the same $\mathrm{FMSS}_{0.75}$ (0.80) with a
less accurate mean, because D-Unet+SDA is over-confident (SS 0.56), a consequence of the
tempered guidance of its sampler. The score thus separates the two components D-Unet+SDA
composes: an amortised mean, accurate, and an explicit-likelihood sampler whose
uncertainty is too narrow. On QG, the learned prior alone, guided from pure noise, is
already competitive with a perfect model (EV 0.9942, CRPSS 0.928, Table~\ref{tab:s0}); the
composition adds little to the mean (0.9951) and makes the distribution markedly
over-confident (SS 0.37 against 0.73). Under model error, however, it protects the estimate
from the misspecified likelihood of the guided sampler (EV 0.9897 against 0.9728,
Table~\ref{tab:s1}). \todo{L63.}

\paragraph{Generalisation beyond the training observing systems.}
Figure~\ref{fig:obs} shows how the schemes respond to the observing system. Within the
range seen in training, the neural schemes follow the same trends as DA and outperform
it as soon as a window holds about twenty observation times; with ten or fewer, DA is
better (RMSE 0.90 for the ETKF against 0.96--1.23 for the neural schemes at ten times,
\Sz). Outside this range, their behaviour depends on how they use the observations. With
fewer observations than in training (6 times), all neural schemes are worse than DA (1.28
to 1.44 against 1.04). With more (1000 times), D-Unet degrades (0.17 at 300 times, 0.34 at
1000) and CFM to a lesser extent (0.17 to 0.25), whereas SDA, whose likelihood is explicit,
keeps improving (0.30 to 0.25), like DA. With fewer observed fast variables than in
training (0 or 2), all schemes degrade smoothly and the neural ones keep their advantage.
When the observation-error variance departs from its training value, all schemes respond
in the expected direction, including D-Unet, which is not told the variance (0.36 at 0.25,
0.40 at 0.5, 0.48 at 1.0). Across observing systems, keeping the observation operator and
error covariance explicit also buys robustness: the ratio of random to regular RMSE is 1.06
for Strong-4DVar, 1.15 for the ETKS and 1.21--1.24 for SDA, against 1.32 for CFM and D-Unet.
In the language of the chain, an amortised map becomes misspecified once the observing
system leaves its training distribution, whereas a scheme with an explicit likelihood does
not.

\paragraph{Marginal value of observations.}
The slope of the curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:obs}a measures what additional observations
bring to each scheme. With a perfect model, going from 10 to 100 observation times divides
the RMSE by 2.6 for the ETKF (0.90 to 0.35) and by 3 to 6 for the neural schemes. Under model
error, the neural schemes keep the same curves, whereas the DA schemes start from a much
higher error and gain far less: the ETKF goes from 1.69 to 1.05 over the same range, and
needs about 1000 observation times (0.61) to approach the error that the neural schemes
reach with 30 (0.38--0.46). The DA error also becomes almost insensitive to the accuracy of
the observations (1.40 to 1.46 for observation-error variances from 0.25 to 1.0, against
0.54 to 0.71 at \Sz), as model error, not observation error, dominates it. The filters
still extract some value from additional observations, whereas the hard constraint does
not (earlier runs, not shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:obs}): the RMSE gain of Strong-4DVar from doubling the observations collapses
$7.0\times$ under model error, against $1.8$--$1.9\times$ for the filters, and its error
stays on a floor set by the model (1.43 at 30 observations, 1.38 at 1000). Model error
thus limits what a model-based DA system extracts from additional observations: an assessment
of the observing system through such a system measures this extraction, not the information
that the observations carry (Sect.~\ref{sec:q1}), and the gap grows with model error.
