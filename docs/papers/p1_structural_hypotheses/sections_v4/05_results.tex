\subsection{Error budget with a perfect model}\label{sec:q1}\label{sec:q1-budget}
\paragraph{Overall performance.}
Table~\ref{tab:s0} reports the performance of all schemes with a perfect model. On L96,
the learned schemes outperform model-based DA. D-Unet+SDA is best for both the mean and
the distribution (EV 0.961, CRPSS 0.853), followed by D-Unet (EV 0.950) and CFM (EV
0.942, CRPSS 0.838). The best DA scheme, the ETKS (EV 0.866, CRPSS 0.744), is behind
every learned scheme, including SDA (EV 0.910, CRPSS 0.772), and the 4D-Var schemes rank
last (EV 0.74--0.76), behind the ensemble filters (0.80--0.82). These differences come
almost entirely from the fast variables. On the 8 slow variables, all schemes reach an
EV of 0.96--0.99, and the ETKS (0.988) is within 0.01 of the best scheme (0.995). On the
16 observed fast variables, the DA schemes reach 0.63--0.80, against 0.87--0.94 for the
learned schemes. On
QG, the schemes are much closer. Strong-4DVar has the best mean (EV 0.980), on par with
D-Unet+SDA (0.977) and D-Unet (0.975), and the ETKS remains the best DA ensemble (EV
0.963). For the distribution, D-Unet+SDA again leads (CRPSS 0.912), ahead of the ETKS
(0.898) and the filters (0.86--0.87). In both case studies, smoothing improves on
filtering, and the ETKF slightly outperforms the EnKF. The spread--skill ratios qualify
these rankings on L96: CFM is nearly calibrated (SS 0.93), whereas the DA ensembles are
over-confident, the more so as they are more accurate (EnKF 0.85, ETKF 0.76, ETKS 0.48).
SDA is moderately over-confident (0.81), and D-Unet+SDA, despite the best mean and CRPSS,
markedly so (0.56), a consequence of its tempered guidance (Sect.~\ref{sec:q3}).

\paragraph{Irreducible component.}
The best scheme sets the floor of each case study: D-Unet+SDA on L96 (normalised
mean-squared error $1-\mathrm{EV} = 0.039$) and Strong-4DVar on QG (0.020). It is only
an upper bound on the irreducible term $\mathrm{mmse}$ in~\eqref{eq:chain0}: we have no lower bound, so every component below is a share of the excess over this floor, not over the true
irreducible error.

\paragraph{Restriction component in filtering schemes.}
A filter conditions each state on past observations only, a smoother on the whole
window. Measured with large ensembles, so that the ensemble size does not contaminate
it, filtering costs 0.057 of normalised MSE on L96 (ETKF 0.138 against ETKS 0.081,
100 members) and 0.026 on QG (0.055 against 0.029, 320 members). It is the largest
identified term of the DA ensembles in both case studies. To check that this gain is a
restriction, we run the filter and the smoother on the same L96 ensemble realisation
and evaluate both sides of the identity
$\mathrm{MSE}_F - \mathrm{MSE}_S = \mathrm{var}_F - \mathrm{var}_S = \E\|m_F - m_S\|^2$
(physical units). It holds approximately with 100 members --- an MSE gap of 0.162
against a variance gap of 0.176 and a mean shift of 0.233 --- and less well with 30
(0.159, 0.227, 0.296). The deviation shrinks as the ensemble grows, as expected if it
is an approximation error of the ensemble.

\paragraph{Approximation components.} We can partly assess the approximation components of the different schemes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Ensemble size in ensemble DA approaches.} Going from 30 to 100 members reduces the normalised MSE
    of the L96 ETKS from 0.134 to 0.081 (40\,\%) and that of the ETKF from 0.185 to
    0.138. On QG, going from 80 to 320 members (with a re-tuned localisation radius)
    reduces it from 0.037 to 0.029 for the ETKS and from 0.066 to 0.055 for the ETKF.
  \item \textbf{Optimisation in 4DVar schemes.} With 10 L-BFGS iterations per sub-window,
    Strong-4DVar is far from its own optimum (validation RMSE 0.721 against 0.545 with
    40 iterations). The benchmark uses 40 iterations, at which the solution has
    converged, so the remaining excess of 4D-Var is not an optimisation error.
  \item \textbf{Training setup and architecture design in neural schemes.} Training D-Unet 
  for 1200 instead of 400 epochs reduces its L96 RMSE from 0.380 to 0.340, and three times more training windows only to 0.334: the approximation term of the learned schemes is close to saturation at our budget.
  Complementary experiments with three tiers of the shared U-Net backbone, under an
  earlier protocol (fixed observing system, 400 epochs, one seed;
  Appendix~\ref{app:tiers}), support that network capacity is not the limiting
  approximation term: a larger tier (23.5\,M parameters) brings no gain, while a
  smaller one with four times fewer parameters (1.5\,M) increases the RMSE by 4 to
  24\,\% (e.g. 0.490 vs 0.470 for D-Unet and 0.410 vs 0.341 for CFM on L96).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Unattributed component.}
The excess left after these contrasts is therefore unattributed: it can contain restriction, approximation and structural misspecification. On L96, it is 0.042 for the 100-member ETKS, and it makes
up almost all of the excess of the 4D-Var schemes (0.20 to 0.22), although they use the
exact model and have converged. Their candidates are structural: the mode instead of
the mean, a hard constraint, a Gaussian background, and the restriction of cycling over
sub-windows. The EnKF exceeds the ETKF (0.199 against 0.185), consistent with the
sampling noise of its perturbed observations. For the neural schemes, the unattributed
term is smaller: 0.011 for D-Unet, 0.019 for CFM and 0.051 for SDA, whose guided sampler
approximates the likelihood. On QG, the same holds for the ensembles and the neural
schemes, at a smaller scale: the unattributed term is 0.009 for the 320-member ETKS, the
EnKF again exceeds the ETKF (0.077 against 0.066), and the neural schemes lie within
0.005 of the floor. The variational scheme is the exception: Strong-4DVar sets the QG
floor itself, so the structural costs that dominate its L96 budget do not appear on the
streamfunction. They are not absent, however: on the potential vorticity, which is
smaller-scale and unobserved, D-Unet and D-Unet+SDA outperform it (EV 0.743 and 0.751
against 0.700).

\paragraph{Synthesis.}
Figure~\ref{fig:budget-s0} summarises the budget per scheme family. For the ensemble
DA schemes, the excess over the floor splits into comparable shares of restriction,
approximation and unattributed error on L96 (ETKF: 0.057, 0.047 and 0.042), while the
restriction due to filtering dominates on QG (ETKF: 0.026, 0.011 and 0.009). For the
variational schemes, the excess is almost entirely unattributed on L96, while
Strong-4DVar is the best scheme on QG. By contrast, the end-to-end neural schemes,
D-Unet and CFM, lie just above the floor in both case studies (excess 0.011 and 0.019
on L96, 0.005 for D-Unet on QG). SDA, which combines a learned prior with an explicit
likelihood, has a larger excess on L96 (0.051), in the same range as the unattributed
term of the 100-member ETKS (0.042). This points to a misspecification of the
posterior induced by the approximate likelihood of the guided sampler, even though the
observation operator and the error statistics are exact. On QG, SDA alone does not
reach a competitive estimate; started from the D-Unet estimate, it stays within 0.003
of the floor (D-Unet+SDA). Overall, even with a perfect dynamical model, the error
budget of the DA schemes involves significant approximation and misspecification
components, beyond the restriction due to their conditioning. These components prevent
the schemes from extracting the full information that the observations carry about
the state. As a consequence, these findings suggest that the skill of end-to-end neural schemes deliver 
a better and more robust indicator of the reconstruction skill that a given observing system actually allows:
assessments of observing systems based on DA alone, such as observing system
simulation experiments, may underestimate the information content of the
observations.

\paragraph{Overall performance.}
Table~\ref{tab:s1} reports the performance under model error. On L96, model-based DA
degrades strongly: the EV of the ETKF, EnKF, ETKS and Strong-4DVar falls to 0.24--0.36,
and the CRPSS of the DA ensembles to about 0.2. Weak-4DVar is the best DA scheme (EV
0.561). The degradation is again much larger on the fast variables: the slow variables
keep an EV of 0.61--0.67 (0.86 for Weak-4DVar), while the fast ones fall to 0.03--0.23
(0.41 for Weak-4DVar). The DA ensembles also become more over-confident: their
spread--skill ratio drops to 0.59 for the ETKF, 0.72 for the EnKF and 0.34 for the ETKS,
while the learned schemes keep their \Sz{} values (CFM 0.94, SDA 0.95, D-Unet+SDA 0.58).

On QG, the DA schemes keep an EV of 0.78--0.85, with Weak-4DVar best for the mean
(0.847) and the ETKS best for the distribution (CRPSS 0.819). The learned schemes are
almost unaffected (L96 EV 0.905--0.961, QG 0.964--0.965), with D-Unet+SDA again best
(CRPSS 0.854 on L96, 0.871 on QG). For L63 and L96, the \So{} test data remain
within the training distribution of the neural schemes, since only the model given to
DA changes. For QG, the \So{} observation errors (15\,\% white and 15\,\% correlated
per pass) lie at the upper edge of the noise range used to train D-Unet (5--15\,\%
white, 0--15\,\% correlated), while the SDA prior does not see the observations, which
enter only through its likelihood. The QG experiments thus also test the robustness of
the neural schemes to observation errors at the limit of their training distribution.

\paragraph{Impacts of misspecification.}\label{sec:q2-represent}
The increase of the normalised MSE from \Sz{} to \So{} measures misspecification plus
the cross term of the chain (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget-s1}, hatched red), and its effect
differs between scheme families. For the ensemble DA schemes, the increase is
0.50--0.54 on L96, i.e. about three times the \Sz{} error of the ETKF and EnKF and nearly
four times that of the ETKS, and 0.09--0.13 on QG. The contrasts that reduced their
\Sz{} excess no longer help: 100 members leave the L96 ETKS unchanged (EV 0.358 with 30
and 100 members), 320 members barely improve the QG ETKS (0.838 to 0.844), and smoothing
gains only 12\,\% of normalised MSE over filtering on L96 (0.642 against 0.728) and
6\,\% on QG (0.162 against 0.172). The filter/smoother identity also fails: on L96, the
MSE gap is 0.237, against a variance gap of 0.534 and a mean shift of 0.913. Re-tuning
the inflation (L96) or the observation-error variance (QG) for \So{} recovers only part
of the loss, e.g. from EV 0.809 to 0.838 for the QG ETKS. For the variational schemes,
the hard constraint is the least robust: the increase is 0.50 on L96 and 0.20 on QG,
where Strong-4DVar goes from the best scheme with a perfect model to the worst DA scheme
under model error. A weak constraint, with its model-error scale tuned per case, gains
little at \Sz{} (EV 0.757 against 0.741 on L96), as expected, but reduces the normalised
MSE of Strong-4DVar by 42\,\% on L96 (0.439 against 0.761) and 31\,\% on QG (0.153
against 0.221) at \So{}, making Weak-4DVar the best DA scheme for the mean. It remains,
however, about ten times the learned bound on L96: an additive per-step model error with
a scalar scale cannot absorb a parametric bias. By contrast, the neural schemes are
barely affected. D-Unet, CFM and D-Unet+SDA are flat on L96, and SDA loses only 0.005,
as its prior, trained with the true parameters, receives the biased ones
(Sect.~\ref{sec:q3}). On QG, they lose about 0.01 of normalised MSE, the effect of the
\So{} observation errors, the only part of \So{} they see.

\paragraph{Synthesis.}\label{sec:q2-oracle}
Under model error, misspecification dominates the budget of every model-based scheme,
and the restriction and approximation terms of \Sz{} become secondary
(Fig.~\ref{fig:budget-s1}). The observations still bring information to the
model-based schemes: on QG, a free run of the biased model has a negative EV on the
streamfunction ($-0.40$), against 0.78--0.85 after assimilation. But misspecification
caps what they can extract, and the explicit representations of model uncertainty
available to DA (a weak constraint, inflation, an inflated observation error) recover
only part of it. The neural schemes, which do not run a dynamical model at inference
time, are insensitive to the misspecification of the model given to DA: on L96 they
reach an EV above 0.9 from the same observations, against at most 0.56 for DA. This gap
is, however, an upper bound on what the observations allow under model error: the
neural schemes are trained on simulations of the true system, so the gap also contains
the value of that training data. \needsrun{Neural schemes trained on simulations of the
biased model, with $\sigma = 0$ and 20\,\%; their gap to the rows trained on the true
system measures what training on reanalyses or true-system data implicitly brings.}
Under model error, the skill of a DA scheme therefore reflects the fidelity of its
model as much as the information content of the observations, which further limits
its use to assess an observing system.

\paragraph{Skill of the reported posterior.}
Figure~\ref{fig:fmss} extends the explained variance of Tables~\ref{tab:s0}
and~\ref{tab:s1} ($\tau = 0$) to the reported distribution ($\tau > 0$). With a perfect
model, all schemes lose skill as $\tau$ grows, but at different rates. The DA ensembles
fall from 0.80--0.87 to 0.41--0.50 at $\tau = 0.75$, and their ranking reverses: the ETKS,
best for the mean, still leads at $\tau = 0.5$ (0.79 against 0.75 for the ETKF) but ends
below both filters (0.41 against 0.50 and 0.49). CFM keeps most of its skill (0.94 to
0.80) and reaches the level of D-Unet+SDA (0.80) at $\tau = 0.75$, although its mean is
less accurate. Reporting no uncertainty is penalised: D-Unet, as accurate as the best
learned schemes for the mean, falls to 0.55, and Strong-4DVar to $-1.6$. Under model
error, the decay of the DA schemes becomes much steeper: their FMSS crosses zero, i.e.
the climatological forecast, as soon as $\tau \approx 0.5$, and reaches $-1.56$ for the
ETKS at $\tau = 0.75$. The learned schemes keep their \Sz{} curves. Misspecification
thus degrades the reported distribution even more than the mean. \todo{QG and L63.}

\paragraph{Calibration and shape.}
The $\tau$-resolved spread--skill ratio (Fig.~\ref{fig:ss-tau}) and the shape diagnostic (Fig.~\ref{fig:g-tau}) explain these differences. CFM is calibrated at every $\tau$ and in both experiments
    ($\mathrm{SS}_\tau$ 0.92--0.95). The DA ensembles are over-confident, and the more so
    the more accurate their mean: at \Sz{}, $\mathrm{SS}_{0.75}$ is 0.56 for the ETKF,
    0.59 for the EnKF and 0.39 for the ETKS, and at \So{} it drops to 0.54, 0.63 and at
    most 0.35. The smoother thus buys its better mean with an over-confident ensemble, and
    model error worsens it. D-Unet+SDA is uniformly over-confident ($\mathrm{SS}_\tau
    \approx 0.56$), the cost of its tempered guidance, while SDA is close to calibrated at
    small $\tau$ and over-confident at large $\tau$ (0.64 at \Sz). A calibrated pooled
    spread does not imply a calibrated distribution: on the random observing system at
    \So, the ETKF has a pooled spread--skill ratio of 0.99, yet a scalar rescaling still
    removes 12\,\% of its score at $\tau = 0.75$, as its spread does not match its error
    where the error occurs. Regarding the shape of the distribution, the diagnostic has a
    small negative floor: on synthetic Gaussian posteriors, 30 members drawn from the true
    distribution give $G_{0.75}$ between $0$ and $-1.4\,\%$, and over-confident Gaussian
    members down to $-4\,\%$, with no shape effect at all. Within this floor, the shape
    of the DA ensembles at \Sz{} ($-4.5\,\%$ for the ETKF, $-5.2\,\%$ for the ETKS) cannot
    be distinguished from the effect of their over-confidence. Under model error, by
    contrast, their shape is clearly misleading ($-22\,\%$ for the ETKS, $-28\,\%$ for the
    ETKF): their members are short-tailed with respect to their errors, so that the truth
    falls outside the ensemble more often than its spread allows. The nearly calibrated
    neural ensembles show a small but genuine gain ($G_{0.75}$ of $+2$ to $+3\,\%$ for CFM
    and SDA): their shape carries some information beyond their mean and variance.

\paragraph{Synthesis.}
The $\tau$-resolved scores separate what the mean-based metrics of Sects.~\ref{sec:q1}
and~\ref{sec:q2} conflate. With a perfect model, the DA ensembles lose more skill from
the mean to the distribution than the learned schemes, because they are over-confident,
and the more so as their mean improves; smoothing and larger ensembles reduce the error
of the mean, not the miscalibration. Under model error, misspecification affects the
reported distribution even more than the mean: the DA ensembles become more
over-confident and, beyond the noise floor of the shape diagnostic, short-tailed, and their score at large $\tau$ falls below that of
climatology. The learned schemes, by contrast, report a distribution whose skill,
calibration and shape are unchanged from \Sz{} to \So{}, with CFM the best calibrated.
Their advantage over DA is therefore larger for the distribution than for the mean.
These conclusions hold when the score is evaluated on random projections of space--time
patches rather than per element (Appendix~\ref{app:fms}), which accounts for the
dependence between variables. \needsrun{The filter/smoother identity along $\tau$ (score
of the ETKS's own filter pass).}

\paragraph{Same likelihood, two priors.}
With a perfect model, the learned joint-window prior (SDA, EV 0.908--0.911) outperforms
the model-derived priors of the hard constraint (Strong-4DVar, 0.741) and of the
30-member smoother (ETKS, 0.866), although these use the exact equations. It does not
outperform the 100-member smoother (0.919): the ordering is set by the approximation term
of the model-based scheme, not by the fidelity of its prior, which is exact at \Sz{}. The
learned prior also gives the better reported distribution ($\mathrm{FMSS}_{0.75}$ of
0.62--0.69 against 0.41 for the ETKS), as it is less over-confident (SS 0.61--0.81
against 0.48).

\paragraph{A better prior is not a better estimate.}
Two observations make the point. Training the SDA prior three times longer (400 to 1200
epochs) improves the estimate by 7 to 9\,\% in RMSE, and the true equations, used as a hard
prior, give the worst DA scheme at \Sz. What matters is how much of the prior the scheme
can actually use within its approximation budget. This reading also resolves the
controlled result that motivated us~\cite{fablet_learning_2021}: a learned prior can beat
the true model because of the approximation and restriction terms of the scheme that uses
the true model, not because the learned prior is better.

\paragraph{Conditioning the prior.}
Conditioning the learned prior on the window parameters $\thv$ and forcing brings little
with a perfect model: SDA1, which ignores them, reaches an EV of 0.908, against 0.910 and
0.911 for SDA2 and SDA3, i.e. 2--3\,\% of normalised MSE. Under model error, this
conditioning becomes a misspecification channel when the prior was trained with exact
parameters: SDA2, given the biased parameters, loses 0.005 of EV and leaks the bias into
the D-Unet+SDA scheme built on it (RMSE 0.310 to 0.333 with 400-epoch priors), whereas SDA3,
trained with noisy parameters, is unaffected. \needsrun{Value of the conditioning
information for DA and SDA: the same test windows with nominal parameters and/or no forcing

\paragraph{Composing a mean and an uncertainty.}
In the operator language of Sect.~\ref{sec:fms}, D-Unet+SDA composes D-Unet's mean with
the anomaly of SDA3's guided sampler. It has the best mean in both experiments (EV 0.961),
but not the best distribution: CFM reaches the same $\mathrm{FMSS}_{0.75}$ (0.80) with a
less accurate mean, because D-Unet+SDA is over-confident (SS 0.56), a consequence of the
tempered guidance of its sampler. The score thus separates the two components D-Unet+SDA
composes: an amortised mean, accurate, and an explicit-likelihood sampler whose
uncertainty is too narrow. The composition matters even more on QG: there, the learned
prior alone, guided by the observations from pure noise, does not reach a competitive
estimate, whereas started from the D-Unet estimate it gives the best QG scheme for both
the mean and the distribution (Table~\ref{tab:s0}). \todo{L63.}

\paragraph{Generalisation beyond the training observing systems.}
Figure~\ref{fig:obs} shows how the schemes respond to the observing system. Within the
range seen in training, the neural schemes follow the same trends as DA and outperform
it as soon as a window holds about twenty observation times; with ten or fewer, DA is
better (RMSE 0.90 for the ETKF against 0.96--1.23 for the neural schemes at ten times,
\Sz). Outside this range, their behaviour depends on how they use the observations. With
fewer observations than in training (6 times), all neural schemes are worse than DA (1.28
to 1.44 against 1.04). With more (1000 times), D-Unet degrades (0.17 at 300 times, 0.34 at
1000) and CFM to a lesser extent (0.17 to 0.25), whereas SDA, whose likelihood is explicit,
keeps improving (0.30 to 0.25), like DA. With fewer observed fast variables than in
training (0 or 2), all schemes degrade smoothly and the neural ones keep their advantage.
When the observation-error variance departs from its training value, all schemes respond
in the expected direction, including D-Unet, which is not told the variance (0.36 at 0.25,
0.40 at 0.5, 0.48 at 1.0). Across observing systems, keeping the observation operator and
error covariance explicit also buys robustness: the ratio of random to regular RMSE is 1.06
for Strong-4DVar, 1.15 for the ETKS and 1.21--1.24 for SDA, against 1.32 for CFM and D-Unet.
In the language of the chain, an amortised map becomes misspecified once the observing
system leaves its training distribution, whereas a scheme with an explicit likelihood does
not.

\paragraph{Marginal value of observations.}
The slope of the curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:obs}a measures what additional observations
bring to each scheme. With a perfect model, going from 10 to 100 observation times divides
the RMSE by 2.6 for the ETKF (0.90 to 0.35) and by 3 to 6 for the neural schemes. Under model
error, the neural schemes keep the same curves, whereas the DA schemes start from a much
higher error and gain far less: the ETKF goes from 1.69 to 1.05 over the same range, and
needs about 1000 observation times (0.61) to approach the error that the neural schemes
reach with 30 (0.38--0.46). The DA error also becomes almost insensitive to the accuracy of
the observations (1.40 to 1.46 for observation-error variances from 0.25 to 1.0, against
0.54 to 0.71 at \Sz), as model error, not observation error, dominates it. The filters
still extract some value from additional observations, whereas the hard constraint does
not: the RMSE gain of Strong-4DVar from doubling the observations collapses
$7.0\times$ under model error, against $1.8$--$1.9\times$ for the filters, and its error
stays on a floor set by the model (1.43 at 30 observations, 1.38 at 1000). Misspecification
thus hides most of the information that the observations carry about the state, which
supports the statement of Sect.~\ref{sec:q1} that DA-based assessments of an observing
system can underestimate its value, all the more under model error.
