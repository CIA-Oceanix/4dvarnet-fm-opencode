\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Data assimilation (DA) combines a dynamical model with observations to estimate
the state of a system~\cite{carrassi_data_2018}. Its premise is that the model
carries information the observations lack, so that a model-based estimate should
beat any estimate that ignores the model. The relevance of state-of-the-art data assimilation systems is widely acknowledged to support the monitoring and forecasting of earth system dynamics \cite{bauer_quiet_2015,carrassi_data_2018,hersbach_era5_2020,moore_synthesis_2019}.

Despite these successes, practice keeps contradicting the above premise, especially when dealing with observation-sparse context. As an example, gridded sea level anomaly products from observation-only optimal and neural interpolation resolves finer scales than an eddy-permitting ocean reanalysis \cite{ballarotta_resolutions_2019,febvre_training_2023}. Over the recent years, numerous learned and hybrid schemes have been
reported to outperform classical DA for both toy dynamical systems and real-wrold ones
\cite{bocquet_bayesian_2020,brajard_combining_2021,rozet_score-based_2023}. Three explanations are usually offered: the dynamical model is wrong; the prior,
derived from the model equations or learned from data, is poorly represented; or the DA
algorithm loses information (filtering instead of smoothing, Gaussian updates, finite
ensembles, local optimisation). They are rarely separated, because each scheme mixes all
three, and a comparison through classic evaluation metrics (e.g., MSE, CRPS) cannot tell
them apart.

This study separates these explanations, for the state estimate and for the uncertainty a
scheme reports. We treat every DA scheme, classical or learned, as an estimator of the
conditional distribution $p(\xv \mid C)$ of the state $\xv$ given a context $C$, and of its
mean $\E[\xv \mid C]$, and use the exact error budget of~\cite{fablet_not_2026}: an
irreducible term; a \emph{restriction}, because the scheme uses less information than
$C$; a \emph{misspecification}, the error the scheme keeps with unlimited resources
(a wrong model, but also a Gaussian update or the mode instead of the mean); and an
\emph{approximation}, the error that more resources (members, iterations, training data and
capacity) remove. Two schemes with the same RMSE can have very different budgets, and only
the budget says what to fix. The three explanations above map onto these terms: model
error onto misspecification of the dynamics, the DA algorithm onto restriction,
approximation and misspecification of the posterior family, and the prior representation
onto both.

Our contributions are the following:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item a measurement of this budget for classical and learned DA schemes on three case
    studies (a Lorenz-63 system, a two-scale Lorenz-96 system and a two-layer
    quasi-geostrophic ocean), with a perfect and an imperfect model, through controlled
    contrasts that isolate each term;
  \item an evaluation of the reported uncertainty with a flow-matching score, a proper
    score that reduces to the mean-squared error at flow time zero and scores the reported
    distribution at larger flow times, with calibration and shape diagnostics usable for DA
    ensembles and learned samplers alike; its theory along the flow time is developed
    in~\cite{fablet_flow_2026};
  \item the resulting picture: with a perfect model, the DA algorithm dominates the budget;
    under model error, misspecification dominates it, and the uncertainty reported by the
    schemes absorbs it only in part; and the prior representation matters through the
    algorithm that uses it.
\end{itemize}
