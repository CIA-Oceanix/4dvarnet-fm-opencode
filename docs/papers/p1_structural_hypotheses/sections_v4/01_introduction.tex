\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Data assimilation (DA) combines a dynamical model with observations to estimate
the state of a system~\cite{carrassi_data_2018}. Its premise is that the model
carries information the observations lack, so that a model-based estimate should
beat any estimate that ignores the model. The relevance of state-of-the-art data assimilation systems is widely acknowledged to support the monitoring and forecasting of earth system dynamics \cite{bauer_quiet_2015,carrassi_data_2018,hersbach_era5_2020,moore_synthesis_2019}.

Despite these successes, practice keeps contradicting the above premise, especially when dealing with observation-sparse context. As an example, gridded sea level anomaly products from observation-only optimal and neural interpolation resolves finer scales than an eddy-permitting ocean reanalysis \cite{ballarotta_resolutions_2019,febvre_training_2023}. Over the recent years, numerous learned and hybrid schemes have been
reported to outperform classical DA for both toy dynamical systems and real-wrold ones
\cite{bocquet_bayesian_2020,brajard_combining_2021,rozet_score-based_2023}. Three explanations are usually offered: the dynamical model is wrong; the DA
algorithm loses information (filtering instead of smoothing, Gaussian updates,
finite ensembles, local optimisation); or the scheme does not use all the
information available to it. They are rarely separated, because each scheme
mixes all three, and a comparison through classic estimation evaluation metrics (e.g., MSE, CRPS,...) cannot tell them apart.

This study aims to disentangle these different contributions in the diagnosis of the overall error budget as well as to understand to which extent this diagnosis is method-specific or tied to the intrinsic properties of the considered dynamics and observing system. Formally, we treat every DA scheme, classical or learned, as an estimator of the conditional distribution $p(\xv \mid C)$ of the state $\xv$ given a context $C$, and of
its mean $\E[\xv \mid C]$. The error of a scheme classically splits along a chain of
idealisations: an irreducible term; a
\emph{restriction}, because the scheme uses less information than $C$; a
\emph{misspecification}, because it believes a model other than the true one;
and an \emph{approximation}, because it computes its own target only
approximately. Two schemes with the same RMSE can have very different budgets,
and only the budget says what to fix. Through a flow matching representation of posteriors, we show the same chain applies to the uncertainty a scheme reports. Overall, the proposed approach allows us to assess the extent to which the resulting scores inform the true underlying posteriors $p(\xv \mid C)$ or reflect the impact of DA models' and algorithms' errors and assumptions. Through experiments on three case studies (a Lorenz-63 system, a two-scale Lorenz-96 system and a two-layer quasi-geostrophic ocean), we diagnose the error budget of model-based and neural DA schemes under perfect-model and model-error settings and investigate whether the form of the prior, derived from the model equations or learned from data, drives the performance.
