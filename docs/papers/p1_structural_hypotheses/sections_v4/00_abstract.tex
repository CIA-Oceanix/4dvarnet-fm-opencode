\begin{abstract}
Model-based data assimilation (DA) is often outperformed by estimators that use
less physics: observation-only products can beat ocean reanalyses, learned
priors can beat the true dynamical model, and neural schemes are increasingly
reported to beat classical ones. The usual explanations (a wrong model, a lossy
algorithm, a poor tuning) are seldom separated.
%
We cast every DA scheme, classical or learned, as an estimator of the conditional
expectation of the state given its context, and represent posteriors with flow
matching. The estimation error then decomposes along a chain of idealisations.
The \emph{irreducible} term is what no scheme can beat. A \emph{restriction} is
the cost of using less information than is available. A \emph{misspecification}
is the cost of believing a wrong model. An \emph{approximation} is the cost of
computing one's own target inexactly. Extending the chain to the full posterior
yields a proper scoring rule that evaluates both the mean estimate and the
uncertainty a scheme reports.
%
On three case studies (a Lorenz-63 system, a two-scale Lorenz-96 system and a
two-layer quasi-geostrophic ocean), we compare DA estimation errors and
uncertainties along three axes: model-based versus neural DA schemes, perfect-model versus model-error
settings, and model-based versus neural priors of the dynamics.
%
Within a perfect-model setting, the apparent edge of neural schemes
(30--45\,\% lower RMSE) is largely a restriction and approximation deficit of
classical DA, and it shrinks with smoothing, larger ensembles and more
optimisation iterations. Under model error, misspecification dominates: it
appears in the low-noise score before it appears in the RMSE, it degrades
calibration, and it makes the best perfect-model scheme (strong-constraint
4D-Var) the worst. Finally, a learned prior competes with the true-model hard
constraint in perfect-model setting, but results in a much greater robustness to parameter tuning and forcing errors. 
%
For sparsely observed systems with significant model error, such as ocean
reanalyses, our findings suggest that neural DA schemes can better reveal the
information actually gained from a given observation configuration, and that
progress lies less in more observations or a more faithful hard-constrained
model than in representing model error explicitly and in assessing reanalyses
with scores that resolve their reported uncertainty, not RMSE alone.
\end{abstract}
