\begin{abstract}
Model-based data assimilation (DA) is often outperformed by estimators that use
less physics: observation-only products can beat ocean reanalyses, learned
priors can beat the true dynamical model, and neural schemes are increasingly
reported to beat classical ones. Three explanations are usually offered (a wrong
dynamical model, a poor representation of the prior, a lossy DA algorithm), but they
are seldom separated, and almost never for the uncertainty a scheme reports.
%
We separate them with an exact error budget: the error of any DA scheme, classical or
learned, splits into an irreducible term, a \emph{restriction} (information the scheme
discards), a \emph{misspecification} (the error it keeps with unlimited resources) and an
\emph{approximation} (the error that more resources remove). We measure these terms with
controlled contrasts (perfect versus imperfect model, filter versus smoother, ensemble
size, iterations and training budget, model-based versus learned priors at matched
likelihood), and extend the evaluation from the mean to the reported uncertainty with a
flow-matching score: a proper score that reduces to the mean-squared error at flow time
zero, scores the reported distribution at larger flow times, and yields calibration and
shape diagnostics.
%
On three case studies (a Lorenz-63 system, a two-scale Lorenz-96 system and a two-layer
quasi-geostrophic ocean), the DA algorithm dominates with a perfect model: the deficit of
classical DA combines restriction, approximation and a misspecification of the posterior
family (Gaussian updates, the mode instead of the mean) that remains with the exact
model, whereas learned schemes lie close to the best estimate. Under model error,
misspecification dominates every model-based budget and is not reduced by more members or
smoothing. The re-tuned spread of the DA ensembles absorbs part of it in the reported
distribution, but not enough on Lorenz-96, where their skill for the distribution falls below
that of climatology while their mean keeps a positive skill. Bounding the part of the score due to
the mean shows that, at large flow times, the reported distributions of the quasi-geostrophic case
are scored mostly through their mean, whereas under model error most of the score of the Lorenz-96
smoother comes from the characterisation of its distribution. The
representation of the prior matters through the algorithm that uses it: a learned prior
can beat the exact equations, and the ranking is set by the approximation budget of the
scheme. Explicit error models keep their value for the uncertainty: with an inflated,
re-tunable observation error, the quasi-geostrophic DA ensembles remain calibrated under
model error, whereas the learned schemes become over-confident outside the error
statistics of their training data.
%
For sparsely observed systems with significant model error, such as ocean reanalyses,
these results suggest assessing DA systems with scores that resolve their reported
uncertainty, and representing model error explicitly rather than relying on a more
faithful hard-constrained model.
\end{abstract}
